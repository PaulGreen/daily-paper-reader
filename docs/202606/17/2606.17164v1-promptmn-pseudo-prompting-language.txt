# ABSTRACT 

Prompting has become the primary interface between humans and generative AI, yet many natural language prompts remain fragile: roles, goals, constraints, and expected outputs are often buried in prose or left implicit. In agentic and software development workflows, a misread at the first handoff can propagate through every step, since a significant portion of agent failures stem from context ambiguities rather than model limitations. This paper introduces PromptMN, a pseudo-prompting domain-specific language that annotates natural language with compact, %-prefixed typed directives covering roles, goals, requirements, priorities, constraints, plans, inputs, and outputs. Semantic resolution lets authors write in any order while the model interprets directives by function. PromptMN sits between informal prompting and programming-style pseudocode: structured enough to be inspectable and reusable, yet lightweight enough for analysts, managers, developers, and stakeholders across the software development lifecycle (SDLC). PromptMN also pairs with reverse prompt engineering. Asking a model to restate a desired outcome as PromptMN lets users inspect the inferred roles, goals, constraints, and missing assumptions before acting, reducing repair cycles and yielding a reusable artifact for aligning people and AI tools. PromptMN’s feasibility is evaluated across several frontier models, including Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.5. The models correctly resolved PromptMN instructions, including complex structures such as repetition, conditionals, methods, and a prime-checking task, without fine-tuning. The same vocabulary applies across new codebases, maintenance, and redesign in the SDLC scenarios presented. While large-scale validation remains future work, these early results suggest PromptMN is a practical step toward clearer, more reviewable human-to-AI interaction.

# 1 Introduction 

The telephone game is a familiar version of what psychology calls serial reproduction, where a message changes as it passes through a chain of interpretation [ 1]. In that setting, the distortion occurs between humans and grows with every retelling. In the AI era, generative AI has made human-to-AI interaction a much more common part of Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), with people asking Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic systems to infer goals, constraints, priorities, and acceptable outputs from ordinary language. The first handoff is where this distortion enters: if intent is not conveyed clearly at the start of the interaction, the model commits to a flawed reading, and agentic systems can carry it forward and amplify it at each step, much as later players repeat a message that has already drifted. In daily work and especially across the software development lifecycle, the quality of that initial inference often decides whether AI support saves time or creates rework. LLMs are useful because they can interpret context, summarize information, generate structured content, write code, plan steps, and call tools. Recent models span billions to trillions of parameters, from 8B and 70B systems to larger 405B and near-trillion-parameter models. Parameter count does not directly equal stored knowledge, but it is a proxy for representational capacity: Morris et al. [ 2] estimate that GPT-family models hold an approximate capacity of 3.6 bits per parameter. By that measure, a 70B-parameter model may encode on the order of tens of gigabytes (roughly 30 GB)

This knowledge is not exposed as a clear database that a user can inspect; it lives in model weights as statistical patterns and is retrieved probabilistically. The prompt, therefore, becomes the main interface for selecting and shaping what the model should do. Effective prompting is more than polite wording. It is the practical act of translating human intent into a form that a model can recognize, organize, and execute [3, 4]. Current prompt practice still relies heavily on free-form prose. Guides commonly recommend roles, background context, examples, constraints, output formats, and iteration, and context engineering frames these choices as the work of supplying the right information at the right time [ 5 , 6]. Yet in real tasks these elements are often buried inside long paragraphs, repeated in slightly different forms, or left implicit. This is costly for beginners because they may not know which pieces matter, and it is also awkward for experienced software practitioners because their working mindset is usually shaped by explicit interfaces, requirements, types, and control structures rather than open-ended semantic guessing. The needed shift is therefore not simply from short prompts to longer prompts. It is from treating a prompt as a chat message to treating it as an inspectable expression of intent. A prompt that drives analysis, coding, planning, or an agentic workflow should make its assumptions, requirements, boundaries, and requested outputs visible before work begins. This does not remove natural language; it gives natural language a small amount of structure so that both human and model can reason over the same task description. PromptMN is introduced as a pseudo-prompting domain-specific language for that purpose. It uses compact reserved keywords, prefixed with ‘%‘, as typed semantic directives inside ordinary prose. Keywords such as ‘%role‘, ‘%goal‘, ‘%req‘, ‘%should‘, ‘%could‘, ‘%mustnot‘, ‘%plan‘, ‘%showplan‘, ‘%in‘, and ‘%out‘ name the parts of a prompt that users already try to express, but often express inconsistently. Its requirement labels also reflect MoSCoW prioritization, where Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, and Won’t Have categories guide requirements planning and often map the first three groups to 60%, 20%, and 20% allocation heuristics [ 7]. The language is intended to be mnemonic rather than heavy: a user can write a full PromptMN block for a complex task, or attach a few directives to a normal prompt when only lightweight structure is needed. This design has three practical aims. First, it reduces wording effort by giving common prompt responsibilities stable names. Second, it works as a reminder template, helping authors include context, constraints, priorities, plans, and output expectations without repeatedly inventing a structure from scratch. Third, it makes prompts easier to inspect, reuse, and revise. In reverse prompting, for example, a model can restate a user’s request as PromptMN so the user can see the model’s inferred plan, assumptions, and missing constraints before execution. The result is a clearer negotiation between human intention and machine interpretation. PromptMN sits between informal prompting and full pseudocode. It does not ask non-programmers to become software engineers, and it does not ask software engineers to abandon natural language. Instead, it offers a shared notation for business analysts, managers, developers, and AI-assisted development tools that already depend on structured intent. At a high level, PromptMN provides small semantic pieces that can be combined for a user’s purpose, like assembling separate puzzle pieces into one task picture. The contribution of this paper is to define that notation, explain its design rationale, and show how it can support more reliable human-AI collaboration without replacing the flexibility that makes prompting useful in the first place. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 surveys prompt engineering, context engineering, and prior structured-prompt work, and positions PromptMN relative to both informal practice and programming-style pseudocode. Section 3 defines the language, covering its syntax, keyword clusters, semantic resolution rule, and interpreter contract. Section 4 demonstrates PromptMN recognition by current models through examples ranging from basic control flow to SDLC-oriented scenarios and reverse prompt engineering. Section 5 reflects on limitations and future work. 

# 2 Related Works 

Prompt engineering is now an established practice with a documented toolkit of techniques, such as role prompting, chain-of-thought, few-shot exemplars, and explicit constraints [ 3 , 4, 8 , 9]. Liu et al. [ 4] sort these into four aspects, namely profile and instruction, knowledge, reasoning and planning, and reliability, and note that unstructured prompts tend to give variable, less reliable answers. Li et al. [ 10 ] tie the fragility of manual prompting to expert dependency and format sensitivity, where small shifts in punctuation or phrasing change the result. Beginner guides agree, since a good prompt has to set a role, limit the scope, and fix the output format [ 8 , 3 ], yet they leave this as advice rather than enforceable structure. PromptMN recasts these levers as typed keywords like %role , %mustnot , and %format , so the recommended structure becomes explicit and checkable. 2PromptMN: A Pseudo-Prompting DSL Context engineering moves the focus from the model to the input it receives. Savva [ 6] describes context provision, that is problem framing, information selection, constraints, and evaluation criteria, as a costly input that hinges on expertise and is hard to observe, and argues that this context, not raw model capability, sets the value of the output. Hua et al. [ 5 ]define the practice as designing and organising context so machines act in line with human intent, and trace it over a twenty-year path from human-computer interaction to today’s agents. The evidence backs this up, because Adam et al. [ 11 ] report that screening F1 swung widely with prompt composition and improved when prompts named the task and listed selection criteria. Gibreel et al. [ 12 ] read prompt-engineering competence as the skill of stating goals, constraints, and context clearly, while MEMENTO [13] shows that a model’s own reasoning benefits from being split into blocks and compressed. These findings motivate PromptMN’s intent and requirement clusters, which reserve a fixed slot for framing, constraints, and success criteria. The need for structure grows once prompts begin to drive autonomous, multi-step work. Practical guides cast an agent as a system that reasons, plans, and acts toward a goal with limited human involvement [ 14 ], and production reports favour workflow decomposition, deterministic orchestration, and externalised prompts over one monolithic prompt [ 15 ]. Pattern catalogues and SDLC reviews repeat the same parts, such as prompt chaining, evaluator and reflect-refine loops, and role specialisation with iterative feedback [16, 17]. ReAct [18] adds that weaving reasoning traces through actions curbs hallucination and error propagation while keeping each step auditable. PromptMN moves these primitives into the prompt through ordered steps and plan disclosure ( %plan , %showplan , %trace ), control flow ( %if , %repeat ,

%method ), and explicit data boundaries ( %in , %out ). The closest prior efforts are those that give prompts a code-like form. The Super-NaturalInstructions study [ 19 ] intro-duced a large collection of NLP tasks to examine how language models generalize from natural-language instructions. Each task combines a definition, positive and negative examples, an input, and an expected output. The results show that example-based instructions improve cross-task transfer, but they also reveal sensitivity to wording and a dependence on high-quality examples. PromptMN builds on this by transforming the structure into a pseudo-prompting language with explicit rules and constraints rather than free-form text. Mishra et al. [ 20 ] rewrote 132 Super-NaturalInstructions tasks into Python-style pseudocode prompts with typed functions, docstrings, and subtask calls. On BLOOM and CodeGen models (2B–7B), these prompts improved classification performance by 7–16 F1 points and ROUGE-L by 12–38%, particularly for code-trained models. They also found that comments, docstrings, and structural elements matter, while writing pseudocode requires technical expertise, limiting its widespread usage. Similarly, Kumar et al. [ 21 ] train LLMs to translate natural-language instructions into Python-like pseudocode before response generation. Fine-tuned on augmented TuluV2 data, their approach improves instruction-following performance by 8–21% across 12 benchmarks without altering the user interaction paradigm, and in some cases even improves performance on mathematical and commonsense reasoning tasks. Taken together, this body of work shows that structure, whether roles, constraints, plans, control flow, or traceable reasoning, makes prompts more reliable, yet it arrives either as informal advice or as full pseudocode that demands programming skill [ 20 , 21 ]. PromptMN sits between these, since it is a compact, mnemonic keyword set that annotates ordinary prose and resolves before execution, which keeps prompt structure reviewable and reusable without leaving natural language. 

# 3 Methodology 

This section presents the design and structure of PromptMN. It opens with the rationale for giving prompts a more defined form, then describes the syntax and keyword set that make up the language, explains how those keywords are grouped into functional clusters, and closes with the contract that governs how a model is expected to process PromptMN directives. 

3.1 Design Rationale 

Prompt engineering has been developing from informal experimentation into a recognised discipline with documented techniques, including role prompting, chain-of-thought reasoning, few-shot demonstrations, and explicit constraints [ 3,4 ].Yet the practice has remained largely unstructured: prompts take the form of free-form natural language instructions, with a notable lack of common vocabulary, consistent formatting conventions, or any mechanism for systematic adaptation across tasks and contexts [ 10 ]. As prompts grow to coordinate multi-agent workflows, their implicit structure becomes a bottleneck, which motivates the case for integrating explicit prompting frameworks rather than relying on unstructured free-text instructions [17]. 3PromptMN: A Pseudo-Prompting DSL Context engineering research has made the cost of this informality visible. As observed by Philipp Schmid [ 6], a substantial subset of agent failures can be classified as context failures rather than fundamental model failures, underscoring the need for structured prompt markup. Hua et al. [ 5] trace context engineering from its roots in sensor-driven, rule-based HCI systems, where designers acted as "intention translators", through to today’s agentic pipelines, where LLMs are still fundamentally bottlenecked by the quality of input tokens rather than model capacity alone. Work on building production agentic systems [ 15 ] traces brittle pipelines and non-deterministic failures back to weak task decomposition and agents carrying too many responsibilities, while ReAct [ 18 ] showed that weaving reasoning traces alongside actions drops hallucination rates and keeps decision steps auditable. Yet none of these efforts appears to provide a formal, reusable vehicle for expressing that structure. PromptMN addresses this gap in part. It introduces a pseudo-prompting domain-specific language (DSL) whose reserved keywords impose sufficient structure for prompts to be parsed, reviewed, and reused, while leaving all prose content deliberately unconstrained. The goal is not to replace natural language but to annotate it with typed, mnemonic directives. 

3.2 Language Design 

PromptMN keywords are written inline with natural language and prefixed with ‘%‘. They act as typed directives that an LLM or agentic runtime is expected to recognise and resolve. Statements are terminated by semicolons ( ;), and related statements can be grouped into block scopes with curly braces ( {...} ). Newlines ( \n ) mark the start of a new term or expression. A central design decision is semantic resolution over source order: the interpreter reorders directives by role before execution, so authors may compose prompts in any reading order without affecting interpretation. By relying on the natural language understanding capabilities of the underlying model, this approach relaxes the authoring burden while supporting a more predictable and well-defined semantic interpretation. The vocabulary is intentionally small, comprising forty-eight named tokens plus the numeric step pattern %<number> ,and every token is mnemonic ( %plan , %req , %if , %out ). Keeping the surface area small lowers the entry cost for writers, echoing prior guidance that prompts work best when they are concise and unambiguous, while wordy or vague phrasing tends to produce weaker outputs [ 8 , 3]. The delimiter pair ∞ ... ∞ marks the boundary of a PromptMN block, making it easy to embed programs inside larger documents or system prompts. Such explicit declarations also help an interpreter reliably recognise PromptMN keywords within surrounding natural-language context, avoiding accidental capture of similar words in the user’s prose.

Cluster 1: Lexical Syntax governs the entire prompt. It defines delimiters, block scope rules, keyword resolution order, and meta-syntactic placeholders. Every other cluster presupposes it. 

Cluster 2: Context & Intent (%role , %intro , %goal , %aware , %risk , %problem , %example , %note , %label ,

%domain) captures the business and domain framing of a task. %role hands the model one or more personas so the interpreter settles into the right stance before touching any other directive. %goal states the intended success criterion, complementing %problem (the gap) with a forward-looking target. %domain extends the context layer by scoping the model’s frame of reference to a particular subject area, supplying the terminology and background knowledge that shape how every subsequent directive is interpreted. Together, these additions complete the intent layer: who is acting, toward what end, from what starting condition, and within which knowledge domain. Taken as a whole, the cluster handles the framing work that decides whether the output is even pointed in the right direction before any reasoning starts. 

Cluster 3: Requirements & Governance (%req , %reqfunc , %reqnonfunc , %newreq , %should , %could , %rule ,

%mustnot ) encodes MoSCoW-style priority signals and hard constraints as typed keywords. Lifting constraints out of free prose and into first-class tokens makes them visible to tooling and harder for the model to quietly drop. 

Cluster 4: Planning & Orchestration (%plan , %<number> , %showplan , %trace ) supports multi-step execution. 

%plan states the high-level strategy; %<number> then enumerates its concrete steps in ascending order ( %1 , %2 , %3 ,. . . ), letting authors scatter step definitions freely across the prompt while the interpreter reassembles them in sequence before acting. %showplan forces that assembled plan to be surfaced before any action is taken, which keeps an early misstep from quietly cascading through later steps. %trace asks for a post-hoc reasoning log delivered through %out ,giving reviewers something concrete to inspect after the fact. 

Cluster 5: Control Flow & Computation (%if , %else , %repeat , %continue , %break , %goto , %var , %method ,

%return ) brings structured programming constructs into the prompt layer. Branching and iteration tend to creep into longer prompts, whether the author plans for them or not; turning them into typed constructs trims the ambiguity of English phrasing and opens the door to reuse through %var substitution and %method encapsulation. %return completes the method abstraction by allowing a %method to terminate at any point and pass a value back to its call site, enabling named behaviors to be composed into larger prompt programs. 

Cluster 6: Data Interface (%in , %data %ignore ,%out , %visualize , %diagram ) makes data boundaries explicit. Drawing a clean line between input and output is routine in software design; in PromptMN that line becomes a keyword-level contract rather than something the reader has to infer. %data extends this boundary by declaring named, schema-aware structured collections — records, tables, lists, or key-value sets — that can be referenced consistently across directives, methods, and steps. %ignore acts as the negative counterpart to %in , explicitly excluding specified files, directories, or context elements from the task scope so they are never read or factored into execution regardless of their presence in the context window. %visualize and %diagram are output-type specifiers that constrain the response to visual artifacts. 

Cluster 7: Meta & Lifecycle Operations (%add , %del , %update , %addition , %newconcept , %format , %meta ) treats prompts as maintainable artefacts. %newconcept lets the vocabulary stretch in-session; %meta captures authorship, version, intended runtime, and locale. The original, full specification of PromptMN is demonstrated in Appendix A, and its concise version, as distilled by Claude Opus 4.8, is presented in Appendix B. 

3.4 Interpreter Contract 

PromptMN does not prescribe a specific model or execution engine. The interpreter contract requires only that its specification be present within a context window or system prompt, or that an LLM be fine-tuned on PromptMN for its proper recognition. This loose coupling means any sufficiently capable LLM can serve as the runtime, and compliance can be tested with a small suite of functional prompts whose observable outputs, namely the externalized execution plan from %showplan and the post-hoc reasoning log from %trace , provide concrete, inspectable evidence of correct directive resolution. The language is intentionally forward-compatible: %newconcept lets practitioners introduce domain-specific keywords without modifying the base specification, and %meta provides a hook for runtime-specific extensions. Both mechanisms reflect the observation that context engineering practices must evolve alongside model capabilities. Both mechanisms reflect the observation that Hua et al. 4.2 Reverse Prompt Engineering with PromptMN 

Reverse prompt engineering (RPE), also known as reverse prompting, flips the conventional workflow on its head: instead of crafting a prompt and hoping the model interprets it correctly, you supply the desired output and ask the 8PromptMN: A Pseudo-Prompting DSL model to work out what prompt would most reliably reproduce it. Rather than relying on a single, static instruction written upfront, this retrospective loop lets the model continuously refine its contextual understanding by reasoning backward from results. Femepid et al. demonstrate that this self-correcting dynamic leads to measurable, compounding improvements in output accuracy over time [22]. RPE pairs particularly well with PromptMN. Because PromptMN prompts are structured, typed, and semantically ordered, with each keyword carrying an unambiguous intent, the model has a far cleaner signal to reason from than it would with free-form prose. Tracing which directive produced which part of the output becomes straightforward, making the backward-reasoning loop faster, more precise, and more actionable. # 5 Conclusion 

PromptMN proposes a compact domain-specific language for a problem that now sits at the centre of human-to-AI interaction: how to express intent clearly enough that a generative model can act on it, preserve it, and expose how it was interpreted. Prompting is no longer only a matter of writing persuasive natural-language instructions. In agentic and software-development settings, a prompt increasingly behaves like an executable handoff. It carries role, goal, context, constraints, priorities, data boundaries, planning steps, and expected outputs. When these elements remain buried in free-form prose, an early misunderstanding can move through the workflow before a human can see where the drift began. The contribution of PromptMN is to make that handoff explicit without asking users to become programmers. Its

Impact on Human-to-AI Interaction 

Typed, semantically ordered directives give a model a cleaner signal than loose prose. They make it easier to see what the user meant and which part of the instruction shaped which part of the response. %showplan externalizes the assembled plan before work begins, while %trace can return a reasoning log afterward. Together, they create review points at both ends of the exchange. Because the vocabulary is small and reads as plain annotation, PromptMN is intended to make structured intent more approachable beyond specialist users, including analysts, managers, developers, and everyday users of generative tools. 

Practical Implications for the SDLC 

PromptMN fits software development especially well because SDLC professionals already think in structured units such as requirements, constraints, plans, interfaces, and outputs. At the same time, the notation lowers the entry cost for less technical stakeholders because it works like a lightweight template for what a good prompt should contain. In the scenarios examined here, the same vocabulary described a fresh codebase, an update to an existing system, an architectural redesign, an SRS-style prompt, and a mixed natural-language request with only a few directives attached. A full PromptMN block can guide a complex task, while single directives such as %aware , %mustnot , or %showplan 

can strengthen an ordinary request when a full block would be unnecessary. The demonstrated results indicate that PromptMN is already recognizable to current advanced models. Empirical testing indicates that contemporary models, such as Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 4.8, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and GPT-5.5, reliably parse and execute PromptMN directives out of the box, and this confirms that the syntax is viable without the overhead of specialized fine-tuning. The Snake game example further shows how a surfaced plan can be reviewed before code or state changes are produced. For AI-assisted development, this matters because early visibility can reduce needless iteration and prevent an incorrect interpretation from becoming rework later in the chain. PromptMN also pairs naturally with reverse prompt engineering. When a model restates a desired outcome as a PromptMN prompt, the result becomes a structured artifact that separates role, goal, requirements, constraints, format, and output expectations. Comparing a user’s original prompt with the model’s PromptMN-style reverse prompt can reveal missing assumptions, weak framing, or constraints the model failed to infer. This makes reverse prompting more useful when the prompt will be reused by another person, another model, or an agentic workflow. 

Limitations and Future Work 

The results in this paper should be read as demonstrative rather than conclusive. PromptMN has been shown to work in selected examples and SDLC-oriented scenarios, but it has not yet been validated through a controlled benchmark, a user study, or a large-scale comparison against plain prompting, SRS-style prompting, and programming-style pseudocode prompting. Its current interpreter is also probabilistic. Unless a model is fine-tuned or a runtime enforces the specification, correct interpretation depends on the model, the context window, and the prompt environment. Prompt injection is also worth noting: if PromptMN directives appear in a system prompt and user input is concatenated into the same context, a user could insert keywords to alter the intended behavior. Input sanitization and clear separation between system and user context are practical mitigations that future work should evaluate. There is also a practical cost. If the full PromptMN specification in Appendix A must be attached to every prompt, it consumes additional tokens. Possible responses include fine-tuning a model on the specification, using a concise model-facing version in Appendix B, or building a linker that attaches only the definitions of the keywords used in a particular prompt. These approaches could reduce token overhead, but they depend on model capability and should be evaluated carefully rather than assumed to be equivalent to the full specification. Future work should therefore focus on formalization, measurement, and tooling. PromptMN needs a grammar, parser, and linter so that structural errors can be detected before a prompt reaches the model. Its effectiveness should be measured across models, domains, prompt lengths, and user groups, using metrics such as task success, constraint following, output variance, time saved, and user effort. Since PromptMN is introduced here as a single-author proposal, its further development should also remain open to contributions from researchers, practitioners, and users who can test, adapt, and improve it in real workflows. Practical tooling should bring PromptMN into everyday use through syntax highlighting, prompt validation, prompt diffing, reverse-prompt support, and tested token-saving strategies such as fine-tuned interpreters, concise specifications, and keyword linkers. 10 PromptMN: A Pseudo-Prompting DSL Overall, PromptMN is best understood as a communication layer between free-form natural language and formal programming. Its promise is not that it removes uncertainty from generative AI, but that it makes intent, structure, and review points more visible. For human-to-AI interaction in general, and for SDLC professionals in particular, that visibility turns prompting from a fragile paragraph into a compact, inspectable, and reusable instruction artifact.

# A Appendix. The specification of the PromptMN 

PromptMN is a pseudo -prompting language (domain - specific language) designed to provide typed, semantic directives for AI prompt interpretation and execution. Version: 1.0.0, Author: Enkhzol Dovdon, License: MIT License, Copyright (c) 2026 Enkhzol Dovdon PromptMN defines a set of reserved keywords that function as interpreter directives. These directives govern how an AI model interprets, organises, and executes prompt instructions. PromptMN annotates prompts using keywords written inline with prose, prefixed with %, and resolved semantically before execution. This allows authors to compose prompts in any reading order without affecting interpretation. The delimiter pair ∞ . . . ∞ marks the program boundary and encloses the language’s complete token set. Within this boundary, tokens are separated by commas (,), and every listed token carries a distinct meaning that the interpreter must correctly recognise and apply. Keywords without an explicit format use the default forms %keyword <text>; or %keyword {statements;}.

The keywords are organised into seven contextual clusters. Cluster 1 applies to the entire prompt as its foundation. 

Cluster 1. Lexical Syntax (Foundational) 

%keyword is generic notation for a unique command that provides additional instructional information. Keywords may appear multiple times and in any position within a prompt. Therefore, the interpreter should logically reorder keywords, resolving them by semantics rather than source order, before executing or following their instructions. Where the syntax is non-obvious, a Format and a For example line follow the definition; otherwise, the default keyword forms given above apply. Semicolon (;) completes an expression statement. Curly braces ({ ... }) define a block scope by grouping a sequence of statements that belong to the command that immediately precedes them; they are written and used as the pair { ... }, and the entries { and } in the enumeration above denote the opening and closing of that pair. Format: %keyword {statement; statement; ...} \n (newline) indicates a line break. After \n, a new term or expression begins. <condition> represents a condition that can be evaluated as true or false, or as satisfied or not satisfied. Angle-bracket tokens such as <condition>, <name>, or <expression> are meta-syntactic placeholders and are not themselves registered keywords.

E.4 Non-Functional Requirements NFR-001 (Platform independence): The deliverable shall run unmodified on current Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge on desktop and mobile, opened from file:// or any static host, with zero installation. 

NFR-002 (Dependencies): Zero third-party code or assets. Only standard web platform APIs (Canvas 2D, Web Audio, Gamepad, localStorage, requestAnimationFrame). 

NFR-003 (Performance): Stable 60 FPS rendering with ≤ 4 ms average simulation time per frame on a 2018-era mid-range laptop; no per-frame allocations in hot loops where avoidable (object pooling for projectiles and particles). 

NFR-004 (Size): The single file should remain under 200 KB uncompressed. 

NFR-005 (Code quality): Code shall be organized in clearly delimited sections (CONFIG, AUDIO, INPUT, PHYSICS, ENTITIES, LEVELS, STATES, RENDER, MAIN), with all tunable constants in CONFIG, and concise comments on every non-obvious algorithm (collision resolution, fixed timestep, audio synthesis). 

NFR-006 (Robustness): No uncaught exceptions during any state transition, window resize, rapid input mashing, or storage failure. Resizing the window at any time shall rescale the canvas without breaking gameplay. 

NFR-007 (Determinism): Given identical inputs, simulation behavior shall be frame-rate independent (consequence of FR-LOOP-001/003). 

NFR-008 (Accessibility, minimum): Color choices shall keep enemies and hazards distinguishable by shape/silhou-ette, not color alone; screen shake shall be subtle and brief.
