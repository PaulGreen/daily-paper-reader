HUIFENG GUO, Huawei Noah’s Ark Lab, China Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) enhance user experience through multi-turn interactions, yet accurately evaluating their performance remains challenging. Recently, employing Large Language Model (LLM) based user simulators for evaluation becomes an effective approach. However, existing LLM-based user simulators suffer from three key limitations: (1) Lack of Domain Adaptability: The simulators’ heavy reliance on fixed prompts and predefined action spaces hinders their ability to adapt and transfer to novel application domains; (2) Limited User Modeling Capability: The simulators lack the capacity for fine-grained user modeling, particularly in accurately replicating subtle linguistic styles and dynamic user preferences; (3) Insufficient Evaluation Validity for Downstream CRSs: When evaluating CRSs, the existing simulators provide not only an unreasonable assessment of fundamental capabilities but also an insufficient examination of system robustness. To overcome these limitations, we propose AdaptSim , an Adaptive domain and automatic prompt tuning User Sim ulator. AdaptSim offers an efficient and comprehensive framework for evaluating the fundamental capabilities and robustness of CRSs by enabling realistic behavior modeling and diverse style generation. AdaptSim leverages automatic prompt generation and optimization to reduce manual effort in domain adapta-tion, and introduces an open action generation mechanism to improve flexibility across domains. For response generation, we employ controlled text generation combined with a “think-then-respond” strategy, enabling fine-grained control over user behavior and language style. For evaluation of CRSs, AdaptSim incorporates a novel framework centered around a Breadth-First Search (BFS)-based, turn-level pairwise comparison to facilitate a comprehensive and rigorous assessment of the basic capabilities and robustness of CRSs.

1 Introduction 

Conversational Recommender Systems. Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) engage users in multi-turn natural language interactions to dynamically capture their preferences and real-time needs, and then provide item recommendations that match their interests [ 4 , 11 , 22 ]. They can respond to user queries instantly at any time and significantly reduce manual workloads, thereby playing a crucial role in real-world applications across various domains, such as e-commerce [ 19 ], online video platforms [ 5 ], and travel services [ 18 ]. However, the construction and evaluation of a CRS still face numerous challenges due to its dynamic and interactive nature [4, 10]. 

User Simulator for CRS. To address this issue, user simulators have emerged as promising solutions recently [ 14 , 22 , 30 ]. To effectively support diverse application scenarios, a user simulator should possess three key characteristics as illustrated in Figure 1: (1) Efficient Domain Adaptability .It enables the simulator to adapt to different application domains efficiently. (2) Fine-grained User Modeling . It allows the simulator to achieve realistic role-playing and accurate performance evaluation. (3) Sufficient Evaluation Validity . It ensures the simulator provides a comprehensive and rigorous assessment of the basic capabilities and robustness of CRSs. The recent remarkable performance of large language models (LLMs) in dialogue comprehension, text generation, and role-playing has driven the rapid development of LLM-based user simulations. These techniques encompass diverse paradigms, ranging from single-prompt methods [ 3 , 24 , 35 ] to agent-based architectures [2, 37]. 

Remaining Limitations. Although current user simulation approaches have made significant progress, they still face several critical limitations: (1) Lack of Domain Adaptability . These methods typically rely on manually crafted fixed prompt templates [ 3 ] and a predefined, limited action space [ 2 , 24 , 37 ], both of which often require careful design and maintenance by domain experts. This not only incurs high development costs but also significantly restricts the simulator’s domain adaptation capability. Consequently, they lack the flexibility and scalability to efficiently adapt to new domains or diverse dialogue contexts in real-world applications. (2) Limited User Modeling Capability. Existing methods [2, 3, 24, 37] generally lack fine-grained modeling of users’ linguistic behavior. The generated responses often remain at a superficial semantic level, failing to capture deeper linguistic attributes such as style, emotion, and politeness strategies, thereby undermining the realism and interactivity of simulated dialogues. One attempt [ 2 ]aims to improve language personalization through style control mechanisms. However, its reliance on refine-based post-processing frameworks disrupts contextual coherence, leading to character inconsistency and illogical transitions. (3) Insufficient Evaluation Validity. Evaluating CRSs with ground-truth dialogues [ 3, 24 , 33 ] is often impractical due to data scarcity. The limitation led to a shift towards using LLMs to simulate user

Our Proposed Method. To address the above limitations, we propose AdaptSim, a high-quality user simulator capable of rapid domain adaptation and the generation of diverse linguistic styles. As shown in Table 1, AdaptSim enables comprehensive evaluation of the basic capabilities of CRS and its robustness under varying stylistic conditions. Specifically, AdaptSim addresses the aforementioned three challenges through the following four key mechanisms: (1) Automatic Prompt Optimization. By leveraging domain-specific knowledge and historical interaction data, it automatically refines prompts to enable the simulator to generate high-quality dialogues across all domains. (2) Open-ended Action Generation. AdaptSim moves away from relying on a set of predefined actions and instead adopts an open-ended action generation mechanism, dynamically constructing user actions based on context, which significantly boosts the simulator’s flexibility across new domains. (3) Thinking-Enhanced Style Generation. To enhance fine-grained control over linguistic behavior of users, we adopt a “think-then-respond” structure in response generation. This method guides the model to think on its language style before generating a response, significantly improving stylistic consistency while maintaining contextual coherence. (4) BFS-based Turn-level Pairwise Evaluation. To achieve an unbiased and fine-grained turn-level assessment of a CRS’s basic capabilities and robustness, this method combines three key elements. It leverages a Breadth-First Search (BFS) to ensure contextual consistency, while the turn-level analysis provides detailed feedback, and the pairwise comparison framework guarantees objectivity.

2 Related Work 

In this section, we provide a literature review of the research about conversational recommendation systems, LLM based user simulator in CRS and automatic prompt optimization. 

2.1 Conversational Recommendation Systems 

Conversational recommender systems (CRSs) aim to support recommendation through interactive natural language exchanges, where the system gradually elicits user preferences and provides personalized suggestions [ 31 ]. Existing CRS methods are commonly grouped into attribute-based and generation-based paradigms [ 12 ]. Attribute-based systems usually guide conversations through predefined slots or templates, which makes their behavior easier to control but limits conver-sational flexibility. Generation-based systems relax these constraints and produce more natural responses [ 25 ], yet traditional generation models often struggle with long-context understanding, preference tracking, and robust dialogue planning. Recent work has explored LLMs as a new foundation for CRSs. ZSCRS [ 8 ] studies the zero-shot recommendation ability of LLMs in conversational settings, while ChatRec [ 6 ] and InteRecA-gent [ 9 ] combine LLMs with external recommendation models or tools to improve interactive recommendation. To further strengthen dialogue management, MACRS [ 3 ] introduces a multi-agent framework for strategic planning in multi-turn recommendation. These studies show the potential of LLM-based CRSs, but they also make evaluation more challenging: system responses become more open-ended, dialogue trajectories diverge quickly, and performance depends on both recommendation quality and conversational behavior. Therefore, reliable user simulation and fine-grained evaluation protocols are increasingly important for assessing CRS capabilities under realistic interaction conditions.

2.2 LLM Based User Simulator in CRS 

In the study of user simulation in CRS leveraging Large Language Models, existing methods can be broadly categorized into two main paradigms: single-prompt frameworks and agent-based frameworks. Early works, such as iEvaLM [ 24 ], MACRS [ 3 ], and SimpleUserSim [ 3], typically adopt the single-prompt paradigm. These approaches rely on manually designed static templates to consolidate user history, candidate items, and task instructions into a single prompt, thereby generating a simulated user response in a single pass. However, the inherent rigidity of this approach leads to several limitations. First, because the generation process is heavily constrained by a fixed template structure, the resulting dialogue flows exhibit limited diversity and often fall into predictable and repetitive patterns. Second, this one-shot generation mechanism makes fine-grained control over the response content challenging, hindering the ability to dynamically adjust the simulated user’s intent or emotional state. Finally, these methods typically rely on ground-truth labels to evaluate the dialogue system, which places emphasis solely on the system’s recommendation accuracy while overlooking its conversational competence. Moreover, ground-truth data are scarce in most real-world scenarios, further constraining the applicability and scalability of such approaches. To overcome the aforementioned limitations, researchers have shifted towards more dynamic, agent-based frameworks. The LLM-driven user agent, CSHI [ 37 ], represents a notable attempt in this direction. It guides the agent’s behavior by explicitly integrating user profiles and a fixed action space. This design aims to enhance the reliability and personalization of simulated behaviors, making the agent’s decisions more grounded. Despite this advancement, CSHI still faces challenges in consistency, behavioral diversity, and persona stability in practice. Building upon CSHI, RecUserSim [ 2] further expands CSHI’s capabilities by augmenting its action space and modeling linguistic styles to improve the diversity of simulated user behavior. However, its core design does not escape the constraints of a predefined framework. Although its action space is more extensive, it remains fundamentally fixed and fails to capture the open-ended and unanticipated behaviors humans exhibit in real interactions. Furthermore, the post-refinement strategy employed by RecUserSim, while capable of locally optimizing response quality, sometimes compromises contextual coherence. This can disrupt the natural flow of the dialogue, thereby limiting its overall performance. In summary, both the single-prompt and the existing agent-based frameworks suffer from a common, fundamental bottleneck: a heavy reliance on manually crafted prompts and predefined action spaces. This strong dependency not only demands significant expert knowledge and engi-neering effort but, more critically, it severely hinders the simulator’s adaptability to new domains or dynamic environments. 

2.3 Automatic Prompt Optimization 

Automatic Prompt Optimization (APO) is an emerging technique aimed at automatically discovering and refining input prompts for language models through iterative feedback and evaluation. Its core objective is to replace the tedious and often intuitive process of manual prompt engineering. Existing approaches have explored various strategies in this domain. For instance, methods like APE [ 34 ] iteratively search for better instructions in a propose-and-score paradigm, combining manually designed seed prompts with feedback from the model itself. Other approaches, such as GPS [ 26 ] and SPRIG [ 29 ], integrate human feedback into the optimization loop, leveraging human preferences to guide the evolution of prompts to enhance their quality and alignment. Meanwhile, to reduce the reliance on human intervention, methods like DAPO [ 28 ] attempt to leverage the language model’s own capabilities for instruction induction, automatically inferring the optimal task instruction from a few examples.

Although these methods have achieved promising results in static tasks (e.g., text classification, summarization), they face significant challenges when applied to dynamic, multi-turn user simula-tion scenarios. Static tasks typically have clear, fixed evaluation criteria, such as accuracy or overlap with a reference answer, whereas the quality assessment of multi-turn dialogues is considerably more complex. The core difficulty lies in the lack of an objective, quantifiable comparative standard across turns. In a simulated dialogue, a “good” response must not only be locally optimal for the current turn but also maintain consistency and coherence with the historical context, while steering the conversation in a meaningful direction. Existing APO methods, typically designed to optimize for single-turn interactions, struggle to capture this long-term dependency on global conversational quality. To address these issues, we draw inspiration from the work of ProTeGi [ 20 ], which demonstrates the effectiveness of optimizing generation strategies via pairwise preference learning in an un-supervised setting. We apply this concept to prompt optimization for dynamic multi-turn user simulation by proposing a novel approach. This approach introduces a multi-round interaction mechanism, where the model generates entire conversational snippets rather than single responses, combined with pairwise comparisons of historical outputs for evaluation. Specifically, the system generates two conversational trajectories driven by different candidate prompts, and then a model or a human annotator determines which trajectory is globally superior (e.g., more coherent or more aligned with the user persona). This design allows the prompt refinement process to move beyond a reliance on hard-to-obtain ground-truth labels, instead leveraging relative preferences for iterative optimization. Consequently, our method not only effectively improves prompt quality for dynamic interaction tasks but also significantly reduces supervision costs, offering a viable path for optimizing user simulators in complex scenarios.

3 Preliminaries 

In this section, we survey the evaluation landscape for CRS. We begin by reviewing the established methodologies for assessing a system’s basic capabilities. We then shift our focus to robustness testing—a comparatively less explored but critical area—to define its scope and outline corresponding evaluation approaches. 

3.1 Evaluation of CRS Basic Capabilities 

Previous methodologies for evaluating the basic capabilities of a CRS have primarily relied on three comparison strategies, each with significant drawbacks: (1) Dialogue-level Pointwise Comparison : This approach assigns a single score to an entire conversation. It is often too coarse-grained, as one score can mask specific turn-level failures and thus lacks diagnostic detail. (2) Turn-level Pointwise Comparison : While more granular, this method evaluates each turn in relative isolation. This can lead to context-agnostic judgments that fail to assess the dialogue’s overall coherence or long-term strategy. Furthermore, the use of discrete rating scales (e.g., 1-5) can lead to quantization errors, while LLM-based evaluators may introduce a positivity bias that obscures meaningful differences between systems. (3) Dialogue-level Pairwise Comparison : This method compares the complete dialogues from two different CRS agents to select a winner. However, as the conversations diverge, the contexts at each turn can become substantially different, making a direct comparison in later stages unfair and unreliable. 

3.2 CRS Robustness 

CRS robustness refers to the system’s ability to maintain performance and accurately infer user intent when faced with diverse and imperfect user inputs. In practice, users often generate non-standard inputs due to spelling errors, ambiguous language, colloquial expressions, or incomplete information. A truly robust CRS must be able to effectively recognize and process such deviations to fulfill the user’s request, rather than misunderstanding or failing completely. To illustrate a common failure in robustness, we constructed a simple test case. As shown in Figure 1, a user simulator intentionally introduces a common spelling error—typing “staek” instead of “steak”—to mimic a realistic user mistake. Despite this minor input deviation, the CRS misunderstands the user’s intent and provides an irrelevant recommendation: “Joe’s Snack Bar.” This example highlights a significant vulnerability in current systems when handling the inherent uncertainty of real-world user language. 

4 Method 

To address the challenges of poor domain adaptability and limited user modeling capability, we propose AdaptSim , an LLM-based Adaptive domain and automatic prompt tuning User Sim ulator. As illustrated in Figure 2, AdaptSim first employs automatic prompt optimization to achieve cross-domain adaptation efficiently (Section 4.1). It then introduces a two-level control mechanism to enable realistic user modeling: at the strategic level, we design an open-ended action generator for dialogue strategy planning (Section 4.2); at the linguistic level, we develop a style controller based on a “think-then-respond” mechanism to enable fine-grained language controllability (Section 4.3).

4.2 Strategy-Level Control: Open-Ended Action Generation 

To enable dialogue strategy planning in user simulation, AdaptSim integrates a strategy-level control mechanism that directly generates user actions instead of selecting from a predefined action set. This approach enhances realism and flexibility, particularly in open-domain scenarios where user behavior may not adhere to a rigid structure. The core idea is inspired by the Bayesian Brain Hypothesis [ 13 ], which posits that the human brain functions as a probabilistic inference engine during decision-making. Following this, we model action generation as a probabilistic reasoning process: the simulator generates multiple candidate actions, estimates their likelihoods, and selects the one with the highest probability as the user’s next move. Formally, given the dialogue history H and user profile P, we treat action generation as proba-bilistic inference over candidate actions. 4.3 Language-Level Control: Thinking-Enhanced Style Generation 

Beyond action strategy planning, realistic user simulation also requires fine-grained control over linguistic expression. In multi-turn dialogues, the accumulation of contextual noise and the weak-ening of style objectives often cause traditional controlled text generation methods to deviate from the intended stylistic constraints [ 17 ]. To address this issue, we draw inspiration from the Metacognition Theory [ 21 ], which emphasizes the role of self-monitoring and self-regulation in cognitive processes. Based on this theory, we design a language-level control mechanism that introduces an explicit thinking step prior to response generation. This step allows the simulator to reflect on its stylistic goals and prepare for consistent expression. Specifically, before generating the final response, the model is required to complete a structured reflection step in the following format:

4.4 BFS-Based Turn-Level Pairwise Evaluation 

To evaluate the basic capabilities and robustness of conversational recommender systems (CRSs), we introduce a BFS-based turn-level pairwise evaluation protocol. The goal is to compare systems under matched dialogue contexts rather than independently evolving conversations. By expanding dialogue states with Breadth-First Search (BFS), the protocol preserves context consistency at each comparison point, while turn-level pairwise judgments provide fine-grained signals and reduce the instability of absolute scoring. 

4.4.1 BFS-Based Turn-Level Pairwise Evaluation for CRS Basics. As outlined in Algorithm 2, we use a tree-structured simulation process to evaluate the basic capabilities of CRSs. Unlike linear simulation, where two systems quickly enter different dialogue contexts after producing different responses, our BFS-based protocol maintains local context consistency for each pairwise comparison. The process starts from an initial user query 𝑎 0 and initializes a queue Q with the corresponding dialogue history. At each iteration, the algorithm dequeues a history state ℎ and asks both target systems, CRS 1 and CRS 2, to generate responses 𝑟 1 and 𝑟 2 from the same history. This synchronous generation step ensures that 𝑟 1 and 𝑟 2 are evaluated under an identical context, avoiding bias caused by context divergence. The user simulator U then interacts with the two responses independently, producing two branches: ℎ · 𝑟 1 · 𝑎 1 and ℎ · 𝑟 2 · 𝑎 2. These new states are added back to the queue, allowing the evaluation to expand breadth-wise over multiple possible dialogue trajectories. A branch terminates when the simulator outputs “END” or when the maximum depth R is reached.

5.1.4 Metrics for Evaluating Simulator. We use the six-dimensional evaluation framework from RecUserSim [ 2 ]. These six metrics were chosen to thoroughly evaluate the user simulator’s ability to replicate real user behavior in interactions with a recommendation system. Naturalness ensures human-like dialogue, making it easier for the system to understand. Adaptability reflects the dynamic nature of user responses. Clarity ensures straightforward communication, while realism guarantees the simulator behaves like a real user. Relevance keeps the dialogue coherent and aligned with the context, and role-play ability ensures the simulator maintains its role as a user, not a recommender. 

◦ Naturalness : The extent to which the language generated by the user simulator resembles natural human language in terms of fluency, tone, and syntactic structure. 

◦ Adaptability : The ability of a user simulator to dynamically adjust its requests or feedback in response to varying actions taken by the recommendation system, reflecting behavior that closely resembles that of a real user. 

◦ Clarity : The degree to which the dialogues generated by a user simulator convey information clearly, directly, and effectively, ensuring ease of understanding. 

◦ Realism : The extent to which a user simulator exhibits behavior that is believable and consistent with how a real human user would respond in a given interaction scenario. 

◦ Relevance : The degree of coherence and logical consistency in the dialogue generated by the user simulator, maintaining semantic alignment and contextual continuity. 

◦ Role-play Ability : The capability of the user simulator to consistently act as a user, engaging in behaviors such as seeking recommendations, responding to queries, and providing feedback. 

5.1.5 Language Style Details of Fine-Grained Control. 

◦ Formality : Value Range: (Formal, Informal) Describes whether the generated language adheres to formal linguistic conventions or is more conversational and informal. 

◦ Sentiment : Value Range: (Positive, Neutral, Negative) Describes the emotional tone conveyed by the language. 

◦ Conciseness : Value Range: (Concise, Moderate, Verbose) Describes the length and information density of the user’s language in expression. 

◦ Carelessness : Value Range: (Careless) Describes whether the language contains spelling mistakes, grammatical errors, incoherent expressions, and so on. 

5.1.6 Metrics for Evaluating CRS. The selection of these three metrics aims to assess the CRS’s performance in language, actions, and recommendations. Language Quality ensures the dialogue is fluent, enhancing user experience. Action Quality evaluates how well the system interprets user intent and selects relevant responses. Recommendation Quality measures how well the system’s suggestions align with user preferences, reflecting the effectiveness of the recommendation mechanism.

6 CONCLUSION 

In this paper, we studied LLM-based user simulation for conversational recommender systems, focusing on the limitations of existing simulators in domain adaptability, user modeling, and evaluation validity. We proposed AdaptSim, an adaptive user simulator that combines automatic prompt optimization, open-ended action generation, and thinking-enhanced style generation. These designs reduce the dependence on manually crafted prompts and predefined action spaces, while enabling more flexible behavior modeling and fine-grained control over user linguistic styles. We further introduced a BFS-based turn-level pairwise evaluation framework for assessing the basic capabilities and robustness of CRSs under matched dialogue contexts. Extensive experiments across multiple domains and LLM backbones show that AdaptSim produces more realistic, adapt-able, and controllable user simulations than existing baselines. The results also demonstrate that AdaptSim can support reliable CRS evaluation and reveal important robustness weaknesses of current CRS models under noisy user inputs. 

7 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK 

Although AdaptSim demonstrates strong performance across domains and backbones, several limitations tied to its specific design choices warrant further investigation. First, the automatic prompt optimization (APO) process relies on an LLM both to detect behav-ioral issues in generated dialogues and to refine the prompt accordingly. This creates a circular dependency: the quality of the optimized prompt is bounded by the meta-evaluation LLM’s ability to identify subtle simulation failures, such as underspecified intent or inconsistent guidance. When the backbone LLM is weak, both the error detection and the refinement steps may degrade simulta-neously, making it difficult to diagnose whether poor simulation quality stems from the prompt or the model itself. Second, the open-ended action generator selects the most probable candidate action, which favors high-frequency, prototypical user behaviors. This greedy selection may suppress rare but realistic actions—such as abrupt topic shifts or contradictory preference updates—that are important for stress-testing CRS robustness. Incorporating diversity-aware sampling or explicit coverage objectives could address this. Third, the thinking-enhanced style controller enforces stylistic consistency through a prompt-driven thinking step, without modifying the model’s internal representations. In very long dialogues, the style signal may still drift as the thinking step competes with an increasingly long context. A more robust solution might involve fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented style anchoring.

Finally, the BFS-based evaluation expands dialogue states breadth-first, which provides context-consistent pairwise comparisons but grows exponentially with branching factor and depth. In practice, we cap the depth at 𝑅 and limit branching, which may miss important late-turn failure modes. Future work could explore beam-search-style pruning or learned branch selection to improve coverage without proportional cost.
