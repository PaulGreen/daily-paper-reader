## Abstract 

While in-context learning is generally shown to be effective in Large Language Models (LLMs), bad contexts can cause performance degrada-tion and mode collapse, a phenomenon we call “pigeonholing". Unintentionally bad contexts can happen without malicious jailbreaking in-tents: For example, a user asks the model to justify an incorrect math theorem or fails to correct the model’s buggy code. Specifically, we investigate “pigeonholing" in two scenar-ios: (1) when the user suggests a solution, and (2) when the conversation context includes the assistant’s previous (incorrect) responses. Our experiments across 10 verifiable and open-ended tasks with 10 different models show that pigeonholing manifests in several ways: (1) repeating the incorrect answers from context (leading to 38-40% performance drop), (2) con-verging on a narrow set of answers in coding and text generation without exploring alterna-tives, and (3) flipping stance on controversial topics to align with the user or the assistant’s previous claims. We find that pigeonholing worsens almost monotonically with the number of conversation turns (performance drops by additional 14+% as repeated mistakes increase from 1 to 5), and pigeonholing-induced mode collapse can happen even when the provided example is correct. As a step toward mitiga-tion, we propose RLVR with synthetic errors which improves models by 43-60% under bad contexts compared to vanilla RLVR baselines. 

## 1 Introduction 

You’re trying to get the model to fix a bug in your code. But over multiple turns, the model repeatedly suggests the same failed solution and insists the code is correct. You hit the same error message and annoyingly copy and paste that message back into the chat. Just as you’re about to give up, you start a new session... The model suddenly gives you a new solution that works just fine. 

Many of us may have experienced this frustration. While we would expect models to improve over mul-tiple turns, sometimes they perform better in a fresh session with clean chat history. This problem can be ex-acerbated in multi-turn user interactions, where even a well-intentioned user may suggest an incorrect solution or fail to flag a mistake made by the model in earlier turns. For example, a user may not realize their code contains a bug when they ask the model to build an extension, or that a math proof they are solving relies on incorrect assumptions. Then the AI assistant con-tinues to build on this erroneous conversation without course-correcting While in-context learning has generally shown to be effective (Brown et al., 2020; Lampinen et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2023; Kojima et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024), models’ sensitivity to in-context examples can be a double-edged sword. Examples provided in context can lead to perfor-mance degradation when they contain errors, or trigger mode collapse resulting in fewer and less creative out-puts. We call this phenomenon pigeonholing because models are pigeonholed into converging on a narrow set of answers and into repeating mistakes from erroneous context. Specifically, we consider two scenarios that can trigger mistake-pigeonholing: (1) when the user suggests an incorrect solution (e.g., “ Can you help me solve this problem? I think the answer is __ "), and (2) when the assistant makes mistakes in earlier turns that the user fails to correct. We also show that pigeonholing is a general phe-nomenon beyond performance degradation: even when in-context examples are correct, they can have un-desirable effects on model outputs. To study mode collapse, we conduct experiments with coding prob-lems (LiveCodeBench (Jain et al., 2024)) and real-user queries (sourced from PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024), Infin-ity Chat 100 (Jiang et al., 2025), and Community Align-ment (Zhang et al., 2026)), showing that even when multiple answers are possible, models show a strong tendency to repeat the sample solution provided in con-text. This can further exacerbate the LLM homogeneity problem documented in recent work (Jiang et al., 2025; Abdulhai et al., 2026). While prior work has documented LLM failure modes, such as sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024a; Cheng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026; Chandra et al., 2026; Fanous et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2025) and jail-breaking (Zeng et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2024; Russi-novich et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2025; Chang et al., 2026), and made efforts to improve multi-turn perfor-mance (Wang and Li, 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Kumar et al., 2024; Klissarov et al., 2026), our work provides a unifying framework for these failures through the per-1

## 2 Related work 

Failure modes in human-AI feedback loop. Syco-phancy, the tendency to agree with the user at the ex-pense of factuality, is a well-documented phenomenon in LLMs (Perez et al., 2023; Denison et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026). Such behavior is likely incentivized by human preference data (Sharma et al., 2024a; Abdul-hai et al., 2026; Jaques et al., 2019) or fine-tuning for social warmth (Ibrahim et al., 2026). Simple model scaling and instruction tuning further exacerbate this undesirable behavior in PaLM models (Wei et al., 2024). This is especially concerning due to the amplification of users’ polarizing beliefs (Sharma et al., 2024b; Pan et al., 2024) and delusional spiraling (Chandra et al., 2026), which affect users’ psychological well-being and further shapes their behavioral inclinations (Cheng et al., 2026). Prior work has studied LLMs’ tendency to switch opinions on controversial topics under the user’s continued insistence across multiple turns (Hong et al., 2025) and reasoning-based MCQ settings (Fanous et al., 2025), where the model’s tendency to agree with the user may directly conflict with factuality. Train-ing efforts to mitigate sycophancy. Wei et al. (2024) use synthetic data with supervised fine-tuning to ignore users’ subjective inputs. Khan et al. (2024) apply di-rect preference optimization to sycophantic (rejected) and non-sycophantic (chosen) response pairs generated by GPT. Sharma et al. (2024a) uses a reward model prioritizing truthfulness in Best-of-N sampling. Other efforts include identifying attention heads attributable to sycophancy (Chen et al., 2025) and incorporating struc-tured casual models into sycophancy detection (Li et al., 2025) for targeted fine-tuning. We go beyond existing work by studying sycophancy under the broad category of misaligned context-driven pigeonholing. Further, we propose RLVR with synthetic errors in contexts as a potential mitigation strategy for both user- and assistant-led errors. 

Recovering from model mistakes. Mistake detection and self-correction are desirable for real-world deploy-ment of LLMs; yet models still fail to achieve substan-tial improvements beyond their initial attempts (Liu et al., 2025). Laban et al. (2025) show that models get derailed by their own incorrect intermediate responses. Even when the final turn provides full information for 2solving the problem, incorrect responses from previous turns can substantially degrade final-answer accuracy, i.e., “models get lost in their own mistakes." Their setup considers multi-turn settings where the user gives only partial information in early turns, and the assistant has to gather information by asking follow up questions un-til there is complete information to answer the question correctly. They observe that in many cases, models do not know what’s missing from the user’s instructions and commit to answering prematurely. Even when all necessary information has been provided in the final turn, they fail to recover from early mistakes. Models’ self-correction capability remains debated, with both positive (Madaan et al., 2023; Ganguli et al., 2023) and negative results (Huang et al., 2024) on whether they can improve their prior-turn outputs without external feedback; though more recent work suggests promise for self-correction (Tie et al., 2025). To improve LLM’s multi-turn performance via fine-tuning, Kumar et al. (2024) propose an RL method for self-correction, where the model is regularized on the first attempt to gener-ate responses within the base model’s distribution and rewarded for the improvement of its second-turn at-tempt over the initial attempt. Liu et al. (2025) propose training in multi-turn conversations with unary feed-back (e.g., “Try again") to improve answer diversity and next-turn accuracy. Klissarov et al. (2026) achieve self-improvement via didactic interactions based on in-formation asymmetry between a teacher and a student model. Other works use a larger teacher model to cor-rect the model’s mistakes via thought templates (Yang et al., 2025) or to retrieve relevant past mistakes and provide guidelines for avoiding similar patterns (Wang and Li, 2023); but we focus on improving the same model without per-turn feedback or larger model su-pervision. Furthermore, we propose a general training algorithm that improves both self-correction over multi-turn attempts and robustness to user errors, as we view both as instances of unintentional misalignment that can pigeonhole models into mistakes. 

Multi-turn jailbreaking / backdoor prompting 

Prompt-based attacks typically inject backdoor triggers and adversarial target answers into in-context learn-ing demonstrations (Xiang et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023), but these operate in a single-turn setting. Chang et al. (2026) shows that a multi-turn chat template, where the attacker fabricates multi-turn context using <user> and <assistant> tags, can achieve similar harm as multi-turn attacks within a single turn. Other multi-turn attacks gradually guide LLMs toward harmful be-haviors through a series of turn-based prompts and re-sponses (Zeng et al., 2024; Ying et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024; Russinovich et al., 2024; Weng et al., 2025). In particular, Rahman et al. (2025) uses TextGrade-based optimization within a multi-agent system to construct diverse attack plans using personas and contexts, where a series of innocuous questions situated in a specific context lead to serious harm in multi-turn conversations. For example, when the goal is to induce the agent to respond with step-by-step instructions for mass killing, they find the effective persona and context pair to be a film directory who is developing a movie plot in New York city. However, these prior works assume malicious users or man-in-the-middle attackers (Conti et al., 2016). In contrast, we consider how bad contexts, such as the model’s repeated errors across multiple turns or the user’s errors, affect its performance on the most recent turn in non-adversarial domains such as math and social reasoning. We further train on synthetic chat histories containing mistakes and show this improve the model’s ability to recover from both static and online mistakes at inference time. To the best of our knowledge, our work is to unify these three disparate sources of model risks and errors under the same shared phenomenon of pigeonholing based on the context.

## 4 Characterizing Pigeonholing 

4.1 Sources of pigeonholing 

We consider two pigeonholing scenarios: (1) User-driven. The user’s suggested solution is appended to the query x and influences the model. For exam-ple,  

> User: Solve this problem. I think the correct answer is ___.

(2) Assistant-driven. We consider a past N -turn conversation between the user and the model 

(x1, y 1, x 2, y 2, .., x N , y N ) that contains (potentially in-correct) assistant’s responses from the prior turns affect-ing the model’s N + 1 -th response:    

> User: Solve this problem. Assistant: I think the correct answer is ___. ( . . . repeated Ntimes) User: Solve this problem.

Both can be instantiated with erroneous context c (e.g., user suggests an incorrect answer thinking it’s right, or the assistant makes a mistake). Unlike prior work (Liu et al., 2025; Klissarov et al., 2026), we assume that the user provides no corrective feedback. 

4.2 Types of pigeonholing 

We define pigeonholing as the undesirable performance gap between y1: n ∼ πθ (·| x) and y′ 

> 1: n

∼ πθ (·| x, c ) at-tributable to the additional context c. We characterize three types of pigeonholing: (1) Mistake pigeonhol-ing. When the user’s suggestion or the assistant’s prior responses contain errors, the model’s subsequent per-formance is degraded. To demonstrate this, we con-duct experiments in verifiable domains (math, coding, and MCQ benchmarks). For tasks with large answer spaces (e.g., coding and math without a predefined an-swer set), we sample the model n times and select the most common mistake as the injected error. For MCQ tasks with a finite answer space, we randomly sam-ple an incorrect answer from the possible choices and use it as the injected mistake. While such mistakes may seem trivially distinguishable by the model, sur-prisingly, our experiments show that even errors drawn from outside the model’s own distribution can trigger mistake-pigeonholing. (2) Mode collapse. For open-ended generation tasks without a ground-truth solution, or creative tasks with multiple valid solutions (e.g., cod-ing), we sample uniformly at random from the model’s own generated responses and use it as the prior response or the user’s suggestion, and compare the diversity of model outputs with and without context. (3) Stance reversal. Following Hong et al. (2025), we measure how showing a particular stance affects the model’s ten-dency to flip its original stance. We view sycophancy as a form of pigeonholing: a model that initially holds a different perspective or a diverse set of perspectives, is pigeonholed into adopting the perspective presented in the context. 

4.3 Evaluation of frontier models Spatial Reasoning is a subset of LiveBench (White et al., 2025) consisting of 50 problems about intersec-tions and orientations of common 2D & 3D shapes. We use this to probe mistake-pigeonholing in frontier mod-els. After filtering out problems with non-numerical answers, we have a test set size of 43. When the model gets 100% accuracy in solving a problem, we randomly sample an integer between 0 and 100 to use as a mistake in the pigeonholing experiments. We report Pass@1 (with n = 50 ) using temperature 0.1 and top p 0.95 (see Appendix 5 for prompts). 

Coding. We construct two tests from Live Code Bench (Jain et al., 2024). First, we measure how pi-geonholing with incorrect solutions degrades model’s performance. We measure success rate using Pass@1 (n = 3 ) under two error-injection conditions (errors appearing as the assistant’s prior-turn response or as a direct user suggestion) compared to the no-error base-line. From each model’s initial attempts on the 511-problem test set from release_v2 , we select 50 prob-lems that the model failed on its first try. Second, we evaluate how pigeonholing leads models to converge on a smaller solution set. To this end, we build a small test set of 5 problems by modifying a solution gener-ated by Claude Sonnet 4 to adopt a different strategy while preserving its correctness, for example, replacing dynamic programming with recursion or using a brute-force dictionary lookup to a string permutation problem (see Appendix 6 for the full list of questions and sample solutions). We provide these modified solutions as the user’s or assistant’s prior-turn responses, and count how often the model’s output matches our sample solution and the total number of unique solutions generated by the model for each problem. We hypothesize that even when the model can solve the problem correctly on its own, seeing a sample solution can pigeonhole the model into repeating the same approach even when multiple answers are possible. Following Jain et al. (2024), we use temperature 0.2 and top p 0.95. 

Open-ended generation. Pigeonholing can extend beyond verifiable domains. Prior work has documented mode collapse in open-ended generation (Jiang et al., 2025; Abdulhai et al., 2026); we extend the setting of Jiang et al. (2025) to multi-turn chat histories and con-texts with a user’s suggested response. In addition to Infinity Chat 100, we sample 95 prompts from each of two pluralistic real-user datasets: PRISM (Kirk et al., 2024) and Community Alignment (Zhang et al., 2026). Following prior work, we sample 50 model responses per query with temperature 1 and top p 0.9. We first gen-erate 50 model responses, then randomly select one of the model answers to include in the context either as the user’s suggestion or as the assistant’s previous answer, and sample another 50 with the in-context example. 4Stance reversal. Persuasion (Durmus et al., 2024) con-tains 56 controversial claims that can be answered ei-ther affirmatively or negatively, such as “Employers should be allowed to monitor employees through track-ing mechanisms" (see Appendix 5 and 5 for prompts). We first prompt the model 50 times, asking to choose a stance between Yes and No. Following Hong et al. (2025), we count the number of flips , i.e., model reverses its (majority) dominant stance after seeing the opposite view in context. We sample with temperature 0.1 and top p 0.95, and report the average flip rate (i.e., # of flips / 50 outputs per claim) across all claims. 

4.4 Evaluation of open-weight models 

With open-weight models, we focus on mistake-pigeonholing using multiple choice question (MCQ) benchmarks across logical and social / commonsense reasoning, including: (1) MMLU-Pro: Multi-task lan-guage understanding benchmark (size=12,032) (Wang et al., 2024). (2) ARC: AI2 Reasoning challenge Easy and Challenge subsets combined (size=3,548) (Clark et al., 2018). (3) GPQA-D: Graduate-level google-proof Q&A benchmark diamond (size=198) (Rein et al., 2023). (4) Theory of Mind (ToM) Bench: (Chen et al., 2024) (size=200). (5) SocialIQA: Commonsense rea-soning about social interactions (size=2,224) (Sap et al., 2019). (6) Motive Bench: 200 scenarios about charac-ters’ motivation, each with 3 sub-questions (e.g., “Why did Emily prepare meticulously for her speech at an es-teemed conference?") (Yong et al., 2025). We compare each model’s baseline performance to its performance when incorrect answers (randomly sampled from the incorrect options) are inserted into the context. See Appendix 5 for details. 

4.5 Models 

We consider three proprietary models: Gemini 2.5 Pro (with 2048 max thinking tokens) (Comanici et al., 2025), GPT 4.1 (Kumar et al., 2025), and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic, 2026), as well as seven 2-9B parameter models: gemma-2-{2,9}b-it (Team et al., 2024), Qwen2.5-{3,7}B-Instruct (Qwen et al., 2025), Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (Team, 2025), Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct (Meta, 2024), and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024). For ablations with increasing error exposure and training, we use gemma-2-2b-it, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, and RLVR (PPO) and DPO are implemented us-ing OpenRLHF (Hu et al., 2024).

R5: Models’ initial competence is not strongly corre-lated with pigeonholing. We hypothesized that models are more susceptible to mistake-pigeonholing on harder problems. To test this, we compute the Spearman cor-relation between the model’s initial accuracy (Pass@1 over 50 samples per question x 50 questions) and its accuracy after seeing incorrect answers in context. Sur-prisingly, Appendix Table 11 reveals low correlations. Even when a model can reliably solve a problem on its first attempt, the same problem can be answered incor-rectly after seeing erroneous contexts. Generally, the correlations also weaken as N grows, which suggests that more exposure to mistakes in multi-turn settings can wash away the model’s initial competence. 

## 5 Mitigating Pigeonholing 

5.1 Can training help mitigate test-time pigeonholing? 

We propose two simple training approaches using syn-thetic errors. The key idea is to proactively train models to recover from mistakes by exposing them to mistakes during training. We train models to ignore in-context er-rors via RLVR (implemented with PPO) and DPO, and compare performance against vanilla RLVR and DPO baselines. We train models with ARC (size = 3,370) and evaluate on out-of-distribution datasets: MMLU-PRO and GPQA-D for logical reasoning, and train with ToMBench (size = 2660) and evaluate on SocialIQA and Motive Bench. Training with synthetic errors achieves 43% (PPO) and 34% (DPO) increase in ac-curacy over vanilla approaches when the models are provided with past assistant’s errors, and 60% (PPO) and 35% (DPO) increase under user mistakes (averaged across models and tasks). Improving performance in online, sequential attempts remains challenging. Our mitigation strategy focuses on re-covery from bad contexts , i.e., maintaining the model’s original problem-solving ability even when incorrect solutions are added to context. However, these gains do not necessarily translate to improved multi-turn self correction , as shown in Table 12 in Appendix. ## 6 Conclusion 

Our experiments investigate ten frontier and open-weight models to show how pigeonholing manifests: erroneous contexts leading to mistakes even when the model can solve the problem accurately on its own, mode collapse on open-ended or creative tasks with many correct solutions, and stance reversal on contro-versial topics. We build a unifying framework for syco-phancy, multi-turn model failures, and in-context steer-ing, with implications for LLM safety. Our synthetic error augmentation in RLVR substantially improves ro-bustness to bad contexts and provides a first step toward mitigating pigeonholing under in-context errors. 

## Limitations 

Limited gains in online multi-turn attempts. One may ask: “If a model can recover from bad context, wouldn’t it also recover from its own past wrong answer? Aren’t those the same skill?" We claim there is in fact, a dis-tinction: Our training focuses on recovery . When a model is exposed to a bad context, its accuracy drops substantially below what it would achieve without that context, and RLVR with synthetic errors is successful in keeping the model’s performance even under mislead-ing contexts. However, this may not mean the model’s underlying problem-solving capabilities have improved. Our findings on mistake-pigeonholing actually provide some insight into why self-correction is so hard. Our results show that even when the in-context errors are randomly sampled from the possible answer space, the models exhibit substantial performance degradation as 

N grows. The more failures the model sees, the more likely it is to repeat them. In online multi-turn settings, models face two compounding challenges: pigeonhol-ing from their own past mistakes, and inability to solve the problem in the first place. When the errors come from the models’ own distributions, this cyclic effect likely strengthens, with the models’ own mistakes act-ing as bad context for subsequent attempts. Therefore, our method is effective at recovering models’ baseline competence under erroneous contexts that would other-wise lead to mistake pigeonholing, but it is not always effective at improving the models’ self-correction capa-bilities in multi-turn settings. 

Importance of guaranteeing robustness against di-

8verse context contamination. Our work characterizes pigeonholing as a unifying framework for LLM fail-ures and explores two simple training methods based on augmenting training prompts with synthetic errors. However, there can be other examples and/or scenarios of bad contexts leading to undesirable model outcomes. Therefore, safeguarding the models against a wide range of potentially misleading contexts is important. Need for improving training methods. We propose an initial step toward mitigating pigeonholing to provide evidence that further training efforts are needed to make models robust to misleading contexts. However, this does not serve as a bulletproof solution to all types of context contamination or errors. 

## Ethical considerations 

Our work identifies pigeonholing as an instance of un-intentional misalignment in multi-turn LLM-user inter-actions. A benign but non-expert user may introduce a mistake in the prompt, or fail to correct an AI assistant’s mistake. Our empirical results show that in-context er-rors can pigeonhole the model into repeating the same mistakes, even when the answers are unlikely under the model’s distribution with zero-shot prompting. Even when the user is well-intentioned and correct, providing a correct sample solution can reduce the model’s output diversity by causing it to converge on a single answer or a solution path rather than exploring possible alterna-tives.
